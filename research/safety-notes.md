# Safety constraints for the supplement planner

Reviewed against official sources on 2026-10-10. These notes separate published guidance from conservative interface policy. The structured companion is `safety-research.json`.

The planner can display adult reference options, but priorities alone do not establish personal eligibility. Missing age, pregnancy status, kidney health, medicines and existing supplement intake must remain **unknown**, never silently become “healthy” or zero intake. If the existing interface has no health inputs, keep a short line in the recommendation card: **“Adult reference only. Health, medicines and current intake not checked.”** An ingredient detail can explain an abstention without adding a health-profile panel. Supplements may interact with medicines or medical conditions, and many have not been tested during pregnancy or breastfeeding. ([NCCIH guidance](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/using-dietary-supplements-wisely))

The selected implementation may generate a reference plan under explicit assumptions: **generally healthy nonpregnant adult, no medicines, other supplements or other caffeine, no kidney disease or caffeine sensitivity, and early-day use at least eight hours before bedtime**. Within that hypothetical scenario, creatine 3 g/day can enter for Strength and caffeine 75 mg can enter for Focus/Energy. Final prototype quantities are twelve 250 mg creatine-monohydrate balls per day and three 25 mg caffeine balls together in one morning serving. Keep the assumptions visible; never convert them to a claim about the real user's verified health or intake. Use a 75 mg/day added-caffeine product cap.

## Candidate behavior

| Ingredient | Conservative planner behavior | Limit and accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Creatine monohydrate | Adult training reference: 3 g/day, no loading. Do not increase the amount as priorities increase. Kidney risk requires clinician review; with unknown screening, show a reference rather than personal clearance. | No numeric population UL established in the cited guidance. Count all creatine products and consistently label monohydrate mass. |
| Caffeine | Optional early-day reference 50–100 mg; a 75 mg serving fits the evidence research candidate. Sleep can veto inclusion. With unknown existing caffeine or timing, abstain from claiming a checked personal dose. | FDA/EFSA: 400 mg/day all-source healthy-adult safety reference; EFSA: 200 mg/single dose. These are safety references, not intake targets or formal nutrient ULs. |
| Magnesium | Withhold automatic inclusion. A 100–200 mg **elemental** reference is conditional on dietary need and screening; Sleep alone does not establish need. | US UL 350 mg/day from supplements/medicines; EFSA UL 250 mg/day for specified magnesium salts in supplements, water or added foods. Natural food magnesium excluded. |
| Vitamin D | Withhold without an established need. No deficiency diagnosis or treatment dose from priorities. | Adult UL 100 mcg = 4,000 IU/day from total oral intake. Count multivitamins and fortified foods. |
| Omega-3 | Withhold routine “healthy brain” inclusion. No automatic high-dose supplementation. | No FNB UL. About 5 g/day EPA+DHA supplement safety assessments do not make that a target dose. Count EPA+DHA, not total fish-oil weight. |
| Melatonin | Withhold blanket daily use. Specific short-term circadian uses require timing, indication and medicine review. | No numeric UL in reviewed official guidance; long-term safety is uncertain. |
| Protein powder | Withhold until a dietary shortfall and an appropriate total target are known. | No FNB UL; diet plus supplements count. Kidney disease requires individualized advice. |
| L-theanine | Conditional evidence candidate only. Do not auto-add for Calm, Sleep, Memory or Energy or describe it as neutralizing caffeine. | No established UL located in reviewed official guidance. Food GRAS status is specific to intended use and does not establish unrestricted supplement safety. |

Creatine dose and protein accounting come from [NIH ODS exercise guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-HealthProfessional/); the kidney-risk precaution comes from [NCCIH performance guidance](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/bodybuilding-and-performance-enhancement-supplements). Evidence from healthy adults does not imply creatine damages healthy kidneys, and the planner should not make that claim. [DoD OPSS](https://www.opss.org/article/creatine-monohydrate-dietary-supplement-performance) describes weight gain and possible gastrointestinal effects and advises guidance and independently tested products.

Caffeine limits, sensitivity, medicines and all-source totals come from [FDA caffeine guidance](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) and [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine). EFSA notes that 100 mg can affect sleep near bedtime. An **at least 8 hours before bed** rule is conservative implementation policy grounded in [NHLBI guidance](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation/healthy-sleep-habits) that effects can last up to eight hours; it is not a guarantee of no sleep effect. Without bedtime input, use “morning only” and say timing is unverified. Do not model bulk pure caffeine as a selectable supplement. ([FDA concentrated-caffeine warning](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-select-dietary-supplement-ingredients-and-other-substances/fda-warns-consumers-about-pure-and-highly-concentrated-caffeine))

[NIH ODS magnesium guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/) supports the elemental unit, kidney-toxicity warning and medicine interactions. Antibiotics are taken at least two hours before or four to six hours after magnesium; oral bisphosphonates need at least two hours separation. A pharmacist should review the actual schedule. [EFSA table and footnote g](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2023-11/ul_summary_tables-version-8.pdf) confirm the narrower 250 mg limit and scope. [NCCIH sleep guidance](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/in-the-news-magnesium-supplements-for-sleep-disorders) reports insufficient rigorous evidence of sleep benefit; do not translate an uncertain association into a universal sleep recommendation.

[NIH ODS vitamin D guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/) confirms the UL, toxicity through hypercalcemia and interaction concern with thiazide diuretics, particularly with impaired kidneys. [NIH ODS omega-3 guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) supports anticoagulant review and notes increased atrial fibrillation risk at 4 g/day in trials in cardiovascular/high-risk populations. Avoid implying routine doses necessarily cause clinically significant bleeding or that this high-dose finding generalizes to all users. Cognitive trial findings do not support automatic healthy-brain inclusion.

[NCCIH melatonin guidance](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/melatonin-what-you-need-to-know) supports short-term versus unknown long-term safety, headache/dizziness/nausea/sleepiness, and medical supervision for epilepsy or blood thinners. It reports insufficient evidence for chronic insomnia. [NIDDK protein guidance for kidney disease](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/Health-Information/Communication-Programs/NKDEP/nutrition-protein-508.pdf) supports individualized protein management for that population.

For L-theanine, [FDA's 2024 review](https://www.fda.gov/media/182086/download) describes oral administration as generally well tolerated. In 19 studies involving 554 healthy adults, exposure was 50–600 mg/day for one day to six weeks; six reported no adverse events and thirteen did not discuss them. This is short-term tolerability context with reporting gaps, not proof of safe long-term daily use. The document reviewed compounded drugs; its recommendation must not be misrepresented as a dietary-supplement ban. [FDA GRAS notice 501](https://hfpappexternal.fda.gov/scripts/fdcc/index.cfm?id=501&set=GRASNotices) is for food ingredient use, not an efficacy endorsement or unrestricted daily limit. Conservative exclusion for unknown pregnancy/medicine status is product policy.

## Interconnected priorities

1. Apply eligibility and exposure limits **before** preference ranking. Never let a high benefit score override missing safety information.
2. Treat dominant Sleep or caffeine intolerance as a caffeine veto; rising Focus should not increase caffeine without bounds. Morning caffeine remains conditional even when Focus ranks first.
3. Do not add melatonin, magnesium or theanine to compensate for stimulant side effects. This is conservative planner policy, not an assertion of a proven pairwise drug interaction.
4. Keep doses fixed within a conservative reference set; sliders change inclusion/ranking. A ball count, if used, must have an ingredient-specific mass and a daily total.
5. Abstention and a partial or empty stack are valid outputs. Explain the missing information in the existing ingredient/detail card.
6. Label generated geometric or weighted scores **preference fit**, not exact percentages of physiological benefit, coverage or personal safety.
7. A dose below a UL is not personal clearance. No established UL is not unlimited safety. Include food, fortified products, medicines and other supplements according to each limit's actual scope.

FDA does not approve dietary supplements for safety and effectiveness before they reach the market. Product quality can differ from trial material. A compact quality note may recommend independently tested, clearly labeled products without claiming that certification proves efficacy. ([FDA supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements))
